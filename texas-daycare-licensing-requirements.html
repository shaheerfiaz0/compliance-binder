Texas Daycare Licensing Requirements: Complete 2026 Guide

Last verified July 2026 · Not legal advice — see sourcing notes throughout

Opening a licensed daycare or childcare center in Texas means meeting requirements set by the Texas Health and Human Services Commission (HHSC), Child Care Regulation (CCR) division, under Texas Administrative Code, Title 26, Chapter 746 (for centers) and Chapter 747 (for licensed and registered homes). This guide covers what actually shows up on an HHSC inspection, in plain language, with the exact regulatory sections cited so you can verify anything before you rely on it.

Regulations are amended periodically. Always confirm current standards at hhs.texas.gov/providers/child-care-regulation/minimum-standards before making a compliance decision.

Who Regulates Childcare in Texas

Texas Human Resources Code, Chapter 42 requires HHSC to license and regulate child care operations, and requires the Texas Department of Family and Protective Services (DFPS) to investigate abuse and neglect allegations at licensed facilities. Operating a childcare business without a license in Texas is illegal and can result in fines or forced closure.

Your program will fall into one of several permit types, each with its own chapter of Minimum Standards — most notably licensed child-care centers (Chapter 746) and licensed or registered child-care homes (Chapter 747). The requirements below apply broadly, but always confirm which chapter governs your specific permit type.

Health & Illness Exclusion Requirements

✓ TAC §746.3601 — quoted directly

This is one of the most common day-to-day compliance points, and one of the clearest in the regulation. A child cannot be admitted (or must be sent home if already in care) if they have:

  • An oral temperature above 101°F, or a tympanic (ear), axillary (armpit), or forehead temperature above 100°F — each accompanied by behavior changes or other signs of illness
  • Two or more vomiting episodes in 24 hours, or uncontrolled diarrhea
  • Lethargy, abnormal breathing, a rash with fever, or mouth sores with drooling
  • A diagnosed communicable disease without medical documentation that the child is no longer contagious
  • Any illness that keeps the child from comfortably participating in activities, including outdoor play

A child can typically return once fever-free for 24 hours without fever-reducing medication, or with written clearance from a healthcare provider.

Every drill must be documented — date, time, and how long it took. Missing drills or missing drill logs is a commonly cited deficiency during HHSC inspections.

Staff Requirements

✓ TAC §745 Subch. F, §746.1309 & §746.1241 — verified against official HHSC PDF

Background checks. Every staff member, contractor, and regular volunteer must complete an FBI fingerprint-based background check and a check of the Texas DFPS Central Registry (child abuse/neglect) before having unsupervised access to children, per Texas Administrative Code Chapter 745, Subchapter F (cross-referenced directly in the Chapter 746 text).

Training. Staff complete pre-service training on a track of 24, 16, or 8 hours depending on role and experience, then 24 clock hours of training annually (§746.1309): at least 6 hours in child growth/development, guidance & discipline, curriculum, or teacher-child interaction; at least 1 hour on child maltreatment prevention and reporting; and, for caregivers of children under 24 months, 1 more hour on safe sleep/SIDS and shaken baby syndrome prevention. Self-study is capped at 3 of the 24 hours — the rest must be instructor-led or self-instructional (e.g. web-based). Emergency preparedness, communicable disease prevention, medication administration, and food-allergy response are also required topics, without a fixed hour minimum. Current CPR and first aid certification is required for any caregiver counted in ratio.

Mandatory reporting. All staff are mandatory reporters of suspected abuse or neglect under Texas Family Code Chapter 261, and report to the Texas Abuse Hotline at 1-800-252-5400 or txabusehotline.org — this number is directly confirmed in HHSC Minimum Standards §746.1241.

What Inspectors Actually Cite Most

Based on publicly available compliance guidance, the most common HHSC citations include: ratio violations during transitions, incomplete or missing training records, gaps in outdoor supervision, and improper storage of cleaning chemicals. HHSC publishes inspection and investigation results publicly, and increasingly, parents check them before enrolling — so compliance isn't just a legal requirement, it's a competitive one.

How Much Does It Cost to Get Licensed in Texas?

Costs vary by permit type, facility size, and whether renovations are needed to meet facility standards, so there isn't one flat number to quote. Budget for the HHSC application/permit fee, background check fees per staff member, any required facility modifications (fire safety, sinks, outdoor space), initial supplies, and liability insurance. Contact your local HHSC Child Care Regulation office for current fee schedules before budgeting a launch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a license to run a daycare out of my home in Texas?

In most cases yes, though very small operations (below a certain number of children) may qualify for an exemption — confirm your specific situation with HHSC, since thresholds and exemption rules can change.

How often does HHSC inspect licensed facilities?

Inspection frequency depends on permit type and your compliance history; HHSC does conduct unannounced inspections.

What happens if I'm cited for a deficiency?

You'll be given an opportunity to correct it within a specified period. Repeat or serious violations can escalate to formal enforcement action.
